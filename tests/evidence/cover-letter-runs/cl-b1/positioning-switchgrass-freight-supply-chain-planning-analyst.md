## 3. The hiring team's view

We came away thinking Dmitri treats a forecast miss as a question with an answer. At Brightwell he went looking for the input the old forecast never saw, found it in store promotions, and rebuilt the forecast around it. Our planning team needs that habit, because our misses come from inputs nobody has named yet, lane by lane.

Our concern was freight. He has planned grocery demand, not trailers, and our volume moves with shippers rather than shelves. What answered it was his time on the warehouse side. He ran cycle counts on a warehouse floor before he built a single forecast, so he has worked where a bad forecast lands. He also writes the kind of weekly note our terminal managers would want.

He'd learn our lanes quickly, because he starts with the misses. We'd hire him for the way he finds the cause of a miss, and we'd expect his first months to go into learning which inputs move our volume.

```text
Subject: Supply Chain Planning Analyst application

Dear Maria Lopez,

Your posting says Switchgrass's forecasts missed by 24% on average last year, and that a missed forecast means idle docks one day and overtime the next. Since 2021 I've been a Planning Analyst at Brightwell Grocers Distribution in Olathe, where I cut the weekly forecast's mean absolute percentage error from 31% to 19% by finding the input it never saw.

Brightwell's weekly forecast covers 1,900 items in three distribution centers, and the old version never saw store promotions, so I rebuilt it in SQL and Excel around them. The first quarter after the rebuild still missed by 24%, and over 52 weeks the error came down to 19%. Every Monday, all 14 of Brightwell's buyers now set their orders from a Power BI dashboard I built for them, and each week I write the buying team a one-page note on where the forecast missed.

Before I forecast anything at Brightwell, I spent 2019 to 2021 as an inventory coordinator on the floor of a 410,000-square-foot warehouse, which is where a forecast miss lands. I ran its cycle counts in Manhattan WMS, and when I moved the high-value items to weekly counts, count variance fell from 2.8% to 0.9%.

This year you added Saturday dock shifts at six terminals and staff them from the weekly forecast. In your seat I'd start with those six terminals and then work through the rest of the 38, looking for the input behind each terminal's misses the way I found the store promotions Brightwell's forecast was missing. With your operations team I'd turn the lane and terminal forecasts into dock staffing and trailer plans before the freight shows up, much as my forecasts become buying plans at Brightwell. Your terminal managers would hear from me every week on forecast accuracy, in the same kind of one-page note Brightwell's buyers already get from me.

I find the input behind a forecast miss and fix it, and I can do that for Switchgrass's lanes. I'd like to talk with you about the lanes your forecasts miss most and what it would take to bring that 24% down.

Best,
Dmitri Okafor
```
