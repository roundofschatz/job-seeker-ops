# Building the case

How to find what the firm is buying, write the hiring team's view and the four lines, rank the proofs and pick the phrases to watch for. The examples here are made up. A finished file is at the end of `format.md`.

## What the firm is buying

A posting holds two kinds of writing. The duties list says what the person will do each week. The rest says why the job exists now: the opening paragraph, a number about what's going wrong, a change the firm is making, and the measure the job is judged on. The firm's own pages often say it more plainly than the posting does.

The case follows the problem. The duties still matter, since they show where the proof has to land, but a case built on them reads like every other applicant's.

**Example.** A posting for a payroll specialist lists the duties: run biweekly payroll, keep employee records current, answer pay questions. Its opening paragraph says the firm moved 1,200 people to a new payroll system in the spring, and a third of the help desk's tickets since then have been pay errors. The firm is buying a clean payroll after a messy switch. The case leads with the candidate's record of catching errors before a run goes out, and the routine work sits underneath it as proof they can run the job.

When the problem and the duties point the same way, line 1 of the case says so and moves on. When the posting names no problem, look for one in the firm's pages and in the measure of the job. If nothing names one, build the case on the requirement the posting puts first, and say in line 2 that the posting didn't state a problem.

## The hiring team's view

This is the conversation the hiring team has after the interview, written as them. Writing it is how the case comes down to one conviction, and how the concern comes out before a reader finds it.

It makes four moves, in order:

1. **How the candidate thinks.** A habit of mind, shown by something they did.
2. **Why that fits this moment.** Tie the habit to what the firm is buying.
3. **The fair concern, and what answered it.** The doubt a fair reader would raise from the evidence, and the evidence that answers it.
4. **An honest summary.** What they'd hire the person for, and where the first months would go.

It runs about 150 to 250 words. It recites no numbers, because the proof bank holds them and this piece holds the reasoning, and it has no urgency line. Every claim in it traces to the evidence. It imagines the conversation, never the facts. The concern goes into section 7 with the phrases to watch for.

**Example opening.** "We came away thinking she checks a payroll run the way an auditor would, line by line, before anyone gets paid. Our payroll needs that habit after the switch, because the errors that cost us are the ones nobody catches until a paycheck is wrong."

## The case in four lines

- **What they want:** the outcome behind the posting, in the firm's terms.
- **Where the candidate stands:** an honest working note on what's strong, what's partial and what's missing. It never goes on a page.
- **The story the evidence tells:** the one or two proofs that show the candidate solving this kind of problem.
- **What the reader should believe:** one sentence. resume-ops aims the summary at it, and cover-letter's last paragraph opens on it.

**The honesty test.** Read line 2 against line 4. When line 4 asks the reader to believe more than line 2 supports, the case reaches past the evidence. Go back to the hiring team's view, find the narrower conviction the evidence holds, and rewrite both.

**Two readings.** Sometimes the evidence supports two cases equally, because the posting names two needs, or because the candidate has two stories of the same strength. Don't pick one quietly. Write each as one line, recommend one and give the reason, such as the one the measure of the job names first or the one with the stronger proof. Ask at step 9, and build the case on the person's pick.

## Ranking the proof bank

Put the proofs in this order:

1. How directly the result answers what the firm is buying.
2. How many required qualifications it proves.
3. How strong the evidence is. A named firm or client with a number beats either one alone.
4. How recent it is.

Keep three to five. When the evidence holds fewer than three, keep what there is and say so in line 2, rather than adding a weak one.

A result from a deeper source that the resume leaves out still goes in the bank, marked "On the resume being sent: no". Mark it for the letter, or for both when it belongs on the resume too. resume-ops decides from the evidence whether it goes on the resume.

The story gives the problem, what the candidate did and what changed, in two to four sentences, all from the source. When the source doesn't give the problem, leave it out rather than supply one.

## Words to use

List the posting's terms the candidate can claim honestly, each with the row or proof that backs it. A term the candidate can't claim stays off the list. Give each internal name, such as a method the candidate named, an in-house tool or a coined term, a plain description a stranger would understand. Pages use the description.

## Keep off the page

Section 7 lists every gap, the concern from the hiring team's view, and anything sensitive, such as a reason for leaving, a pay figure, health, family, or a conflict at a past job.

The phrases to watch for are the words that would put an item on a page, such as "new to freight", "still finishing my license" or "between roles". Pick phrases an honest page about this person would never need, so a match always means a leak. Leave out any word their real work uses. When the candidate ran freight audits, "freight" can't be one.
